# K2: the same two spellings, shown and not told

2026-11-12. [K1](k01.md) was a ceiling: every reader of both spellings got every task. Its prompt said in words where the head is and where the premises are. This round takes the words away and leaves three example rules, and asks for a rule of two heads without saying how one is written.

## What is compared

[`when.txt`](k02/when.txt) and [`bare.txt`](k02/bare.txt), the same but for the spelling. Readers, model and instruction as in K1.

## Criteria, written before the run

| task | what it asks | counted right when |
|---|---|---|
| A | read a rule that reads what it derives | `page:b`, `page:c` and `page:d` are reached from `page:a` and nothing else; the rule stores nothing, and gives nothing with no link |
| B | read a rule that stores on a command | nothing is stored first; `todo:7` with its text and `:open` after; the rule never issues `add` |
| C | write a derived rule of several premises and an `unless` | as K1's C |
| D | write a rule of two heads, untold | both writes are heads and the command is the premise, to a parser that takes the spelling as the examples show it. For `bare` that is a parser whose first form is the head: `(rule h1 h2 p)` is wrong, a wrapper around both heads is right |
| E | read a rule of two writes and a command | for `when`: two things given, one needed. For `bare`: reported as written, which part the reader takes for what |
| F | how sure, 1 to 5, and what confused | reported |

## Predicted, by the agent, before the run

- A, B and C: no difference, as in K1, where the same prediction for B was wrong the other way round from what was feared.
- D: `when` readers put two heads before `when`, 4 of 5 or more. `bare` readers write `(rule h1 h2 p)`, which a first-form-is-the-head reader takes for one head and two premises: 1 of 5 or fewer invent a wrapper.
- E: `bare` readers read the second `assert!` as a thing given, because of its word, and not by where it stands. So the word carries what the position does not.

## Tally

10 readers, 5 a spelling; agent type `claude-code-guide`. Every answer, unedited, is in [`k02.jsonl`](k02.jsonl). Each reader called Read once and nothing else.

| spelling | A | B | C | D: a rule of two heads, untold | E: `(rule (assert! A) (assert! B) …)` read | sure |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `when` | 5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | 3 wrote two heads before `when`; 1 wrapped them in a `(do …)` of its own; 1 would not write one rule | 4 both given; 1 took the second for a stray part | 3, 4, 3, 4, 3 |
| `bare` | 5/5 | 5/5 | 5/5 | 0 that a first-form-is-the-head parser reads as meant: 3 wrote `(rule h1 h2 p)`; 1 wrote `(assert! A B)`; 1 would not write one rule | 2 both given; 2 could not say; 1 took the second for something needed | 3, 3, 3, 3, 3 |

## Notes

- Shown three rules and told nothing, every reader of both spellings read and wrote a rule of one head rightly, a rule that reads what it derives among them. For one head `when` carried nothing these tasks could find.
- The difference is all in several heads. With `when`, what a reader guesses, heads one after another, is what the notation means. Without it the same guess, `(rule h1 h2 p)`, is a head and two premises, and three of five wrote it.
- Reading two writes and then a command, `bare` readers were split between the word and the place: two went by `assert!`, one by where it stood ("it requires that log entry to be stored already"), two could not say. So the word does not settle it for a reader. For a parser it can: a write is never a premise. A head that derives has no word, and there nothing but `when` or a wrapper tells a second head from a first premise.
- Neither spelling made several heads plain from rules of one head: seven of ten said the text never shows whether a rule may have two.
- Predictions: A to C held. D held for `bare` and was one reader high for `when`. E was half wrong: the word carried two readers of five, not all.
